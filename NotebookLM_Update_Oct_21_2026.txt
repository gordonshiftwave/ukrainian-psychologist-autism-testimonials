We use the ten-minute "Ukrainian Kids" program with Ukrainian translation.

The boy had a comprehensive assessment, and according to his sensory profile, there is a need on the side of the proprioceptive system. Plus, based on the results of the neuropsychological screening, it was important to work on interhemispheric interaction. And the chair handles these points (unclear in the audio) well. It works directly with proprioception. And this is important, because he is a teenager (her word), and we can work without direct tactile contact from the specialist.

At the same time, the most noticeable changes for me also concern the boy's state after the sessions. He yawns, he stretches, he feels relaxed and rested, he smiles. And at the same time he becomes livelier. He starts talking more, telling stories, sharing. He engages in interaction.

He is usually more shy (partly unclear in the audio), so this change in his behavior is noticeable.

In this case, the session in the chair is like a small bridge, a transition into further psychological work and lessons. And from what I have observed, the boy now gets into the work of his program more easily.

So for me it is important that the child likes it and says so, and that he shows a wish to do it again.

That is why I truly believe that, with this boy, Shiftwave really is a useful and important tool.

Respondent: Yuliia Babenko
Title: Psychologist, Rivnovaha Psychological Assistance Center, Ukraine
Answered: October 17, 2026
Language of original: Ukrainian (written)

Q1. Are you currently using Shiftwave, and how does it help children and the people who care for them?

Yes, I use the Shiftwave chair in my work with preschool children with autism spectrum disorder and attention deficit hyperactivity disorder at the Rivnovaha center.

For me it is an additional sensory support tool that helps prepare a child for a session, makes transitions between activities easier, and supports self-regulation in moments of overexcitement or heightened motor activity.

Q2. What are the one or two biggest changes you have noticed in children who used the chair?

Most often I noticed that after using the chair it became easier for children to regulate their state and calm down. The active seeking of proprioceptive stimulation also decreased or stopped, and attention and readiness to interact during the rest of the session improved. In most cases there was also a noticeable improvement in mood.

Q3. Can you tell the story of one child for whom the experience with Shiftwave was especially positive?

In my practice there was an especially telling case: a 6-year-old boy with autism spectrum disorder.

When we started working together, the child came to sessions in a state of strong emotional arousal. This was connected to a previous negative experience with a psychologist who used a directive style of communication. The boy might cry loudly, tremble, not respond when spoken to, and refuse sensory games, the hammock, and any play activity. He often went to the door, showing that he wanted to leave the room.

In that state, the Shiftwave chair became a way for him to receive sensory stimulation without having to actively interact with the psychologist. We used short sessions of about 10 minutes, guided by the child's state and reactions.

During the first session the boy's state kept changing: he would calm down, then start crying again. But even then the chair turned out to be the only one of the tools we offered that made it possible to lower his arousal, even if only for a short time. During the second session the crying almost stopped, and the child now sat in the chair willingly. From the third session on, I observed a mostly positive emotional state.

Importantly, the boy refused the headphones, and we did not insist on them. I stayed nearby and watched his state, sometimes in silence, and sometimes telling him, in a calm, even voice, about a toy that had caught his interest, without expecting an answer or active interaction.

After using the chair, we continued sensory stimulation with other tools and games that the child was ready to accept. This made it possible to keep up a positive emotional state and gradually move on to further activities without overloading him.

Now I use the chair before the start of a session if I notice that a child is overexcited or finds it hard to settle into interaction. For me this experience is especially valuable because the chair became an accessible way for the boy to get sensory support without extra social demands. It helped create more comfortable conditions for gradually drawing him into further activities.

Q4. What have you noticed specifically in nonverbal four-year-old children?

When working with nonverbal children, it is important to pay attention to the nonverbal signs of their state: motor activity, muscle tension, the nature of their behavior, their reactions to sensory stimuli, and their readiness to interact.

During and after a session, I watch whether the child's level of arousal changes, whether it is easier for the child to move on to the next activity, and whether the child stays in a comfortable state.

At the same time, being nonverbal does not in itself determine how a child reacts to the chair. Every child has an individual sensory profile, so it is important to be guided by the child's reactions, signals of comfort or discomfort, and particular behaviors.

Q5. What did Shiftwave give that other methods did not, and if you could take only one tool for the children, what would you choose?

Shiftwave is an excellent additional tool for sensory support and for corrective and developmental work. It should not be set against other methods, because each tool has its own purpose.

At the same time, in my practice I have concluded that the chair is especially useful when a child needs proprioceptive stimulation but is not yet ready for direct interaction with another person. It makes it possible to offer the child sensory support without extra social demands, respecting the child's current state and need for distance.
